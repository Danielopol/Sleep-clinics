Search for what a CPAP machine costs without insurance and you get a single number: somewhere between $700 and $1,100. That number is accurate and not very useful, because nobody sleeps on a machine alone. It needs a mask, a hose, a water chamber and filters, and then it needs replacing parts for as long as you use it.

So this guide prices three things instead: the machine, the working setup you actually need on day one, and what the next five years cost once you are the one paying for supplies. The last number is the one most buying guides skip, and it is the biggest.

Prices were taken from CPAP.com's product listings on October 12, 2026. This is a price analysis, not a hands-on test, and prices move.

## The short answer

- **The machine alone:** $873 to $1,004 for a new standard auto-adjusting machine at CPAP.com.
- **A working setup on day one:** about $970 to $1,200, depending on the machine and mask, because the mask, a heated hose and the other pieces are not all in the box.
- **Five years of ownership:** roughly $2,700 to $5,100 for one realistic setup, depending on how often you replace parts. The machine is only about a fifth to two-fifths of that.

That range is wide on purpose. The difference between its two ends is not luck. It is how often you replace the cushion, the headgear and the hose, and it is a choice you control.

## What moves the number most

### The mask you choose

Cushions and headgear are the part of the setup you replace most, and the three common mask types cost different amounts to keep running. Per year, for cushion or pillows plus headgear:

| Mask | Cushion or pillows | Headgear | Longer intervals | Shorter intervals |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AirFit P10 (nasal pillows) | $22 | $28 | $144 | $376 |
| AirFit N20 (nasal) | $34 | $34 | $204 | $544 |
| AirFit F20 (full face) | $54 | $34 | $284 | $784 |

The same machine can cost $400 a year more to keep running with a full face mask than with nasal pillows, if you replace on the shorter schedule. That is not a reason to pick a mask for its price. A mask has to fit and seal, and your breathing decides which type you can use, as our [starter guide](/blog/best-cpap-machine-for-beginners) explains. It is a reason to know the running cost before you assume the cheapest day-one setup is the cheapest overall.

### The machine

The G3 X is the least expensive new standard (non-travel) machine at CPAP.com at $873, and it carries a five year warranty and includes a heated hose. The AirSense 10 is $960 and includes a heated ClimateLine hose. The AirSense 11 is $1,004 and does not. The price gaps are small beside the supplies, and the case for the AirSense 11 is its guided setup and built-in cellular, not its price. Our [2026 machine comparison](/blog/best-cpap-machines-2026) covers the trade-offs.

### How often you replace parts

This is the biggest lever, and the one to be careful with. The intervals above are ranges from retailers and manufacturers, not clinical requirements, and parts wear at different rates depending on how you clean them, your skin and your water. Replacing on condition is reasonable: a cushion that no longer seals, a hose with a hole, or a chamber with cracks or mineral buildup should be replaced whatever the calendar says. Stretching a part well past visible wear saves money and costs you comfort, leaks and hygiene. See [when to replace your CPAP machine](/blog/when-to-replace-cpap-machine) for the signs that point at the machine itself.

## Ways to lower the cost

In rough order of how many dollars each can move:

**1. Choose a previous-generation or value machine.** Setting up an AirSense 10 with an N20 costs $1,065, about $90 less than the AirSense 11 set up the same way. The G3 X with a Nova mask is $972, about $183 less.

**2. Consider certified pre-owned.** CPAP.com sells certified pre-owned AirSense 10 AutoSet machines, professionally restored and tested, with a 60-day warranty. The listing carries a $399 price, but the page tells buyers to call or chat for current availability and pricing, so treat $399 as indicative and not a quote. The trade-off is plain: a 60-day warranty against two years on a new machine, on a device you will run every night.

**3. Pay with HSA or FSA funds.** CPAP.com accepts HSA and FSA cards. These dollars are pre-tax, so the saving is roughly your marginal tax rate times the spend: at a 22% federal bracket, about $250 on a $1,155 setup. IRS Publication 502 defines medical expenses as "the costs of diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease," and the portion we read does not name CPAP specifically, so eligibility is a question for your plan administrator. Keep your prescription and itemized receipts, and note that FSA funds usually expire at the end of the plan year while HSA funds roll over.

**4. Spread the cost if you need to.** CPAP.com lists Affirm among its payment options, which splits a purchase into smaller payments. A payment plan does not reduce the price, and depending on the plan it can raise it, so read the terms Affirm shows you before you commit.

**5. Watch the sale prices.** The water chamber, filters and heated hose were all marked down when we checked, and CPAP.com showed sitewide sale banners. We could not tell whether those banners apply to machines, so compare the cart total, not only the listed price.

## When insurance is cheaper

Paying cash is not always the cheaper path. With a low deductible and good coverage through a durable medical equipment supplier, insurance often wins. With a high deductible, the cash price can beat coinsurance across a 13 month rental.

Insurance also changes the supplies math. CPAP.com's guide notes that Medicare allows one new mask every three months, which is a schedule an insurer pays for and a cash buyer pays for out of pocket. Our [new diagnosis guide](/blog/best-cpap-machine-for-beginners) covers the Medicare rental and adherence rules, and the two numbers to compare are your plan's cost over 13 months and the cash setup above.

## Before you pay cash

1. **Get the prescription first,** because no legitimate US seller will ship a machine without one. [Where to buy a CPAP machine online](/blog/where-to-buy-a-cpap-machine-online) lists what a valid one must include.
2. **Price the whole setup,** not the machine: mask, heated hose, chamber, filters and cleaning supplies.
3. **Decide your replacement schedule** and price a year of supplies before you choose a mask.
4. **Check the return terms.** At CPAP.com a machine carries a one-time 60 night risk-free return from the ship date, in original packaging and free of heavy wear, with return shipping at your cost. Opened masks can be exchanged within 60 nights. Other sellers differ.
5. **Compare the insurance path** against these numbers before you decide.

A CPAP machine costs $873 to $1,004 new at CPAP.com. A working setup costs about $970 to $1,200 on day one, and over five years the total is roughly $2,700 to $5,100, with supplies overtaking the machine's price in about three years.
