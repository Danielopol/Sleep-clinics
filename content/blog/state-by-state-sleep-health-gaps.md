Sleep disorders don't respect state lines, but access to sleep medicine does. A patient in Boston or Dallas can often choose between a dozen accredited sleep centers within a short drive. A patient in rural Wyoming or Vermont may need to travel hours, or wait months, for the same evaluation.

We pulled the numbers from our own directory of more than 4,700 sleep clinics across the United States to see exactly how uneven that access really is. What we found is a clear divide between densely served metro corridors and a long tail of states, mostly rural, low-population, or geographically spread out, where sleep specialists are genuinely hard to reach.

Here's the full picture, state by state, and what it means if you're trying to find care.

## A Quick Note on the Data

The figures below reflect clinics listed in our directory, cross-referenced against approximate 2023 U.S. Census population estimates, to calculate clinics per 100,000 residents. This isn't a comprehensive census of every sleep provider in America, some clinics operate without a strong web presence and may be underrepresented, but with 4,700+ locations mapped, it's one of the more complete national pictures available, and the relative pattern, dense coverage in some states, thin coverage in others, holds up clearly.

A note on interpretation: raw clinic counts favor big states by population. Clinics per 100,000 residents is the more meaningful "access" metric, since it roughly captures how many people each clinic in a state is potentially serving. A small state with just a handful of clinics can still have strong per-capita access if its population is small too.

## The National Picture

Across all 50 states and DC, the clinics-per-100k figure ranges from under 0.5 to well over 3, a roughly sevenfold difference in apparent density of sleep care. That gap tracks closely with two underlying factors: population density (rural states simply have fewer people within a reasonable driving distance to support a full-service sleep lab) and healthcare infrastructure investment more broadly (states with more hospital systems and academic medical centers tend to have more sleep medicine subspecialists).

It's worth noting that "sleep health" isn't just about the number of clinics, sleep disorders themselves are unevenly distributed too, correlating with regional patterns in obesity, shift work, and access to primary care that would otherwise catch symptoms early and refer patients onward. Thin clinic access and elevated risk factors can compound each other in exactly the states least equipped to handle it.

## Reading the Table: What Actually Stands Out

### The Surprise at the Top: Large States, Thin Per-Capita Coverage

California, Nevada, Colorado, Texas, Arizona, and Florida, all fast-growing, large-population states, land at the bottom of the per-capita list. This isn't necessarily because these states lack sleep medicine infrastructure; some, like Texas and Florida, have very high raw clinic counts (295 and 232 respectively, more than almost anywhere else). It's that population growth in these states has outpaced the expansion of specialist capacity. A large, spread-out metro population competing for a fixed number of sleep lab slots produces longer wait times even where clinics technically exist.

If you live in one of these states, the practical implication isn't "there's no care here", it's "book early, and consider a home sleep test if you're a candidate, since in-lab appointment availability may be tighter than the raw clinic count suggests." Our guide on [home sleep tests vs. in-lab studies](/blog/home-sleep-test-vs-in-lab-sleep-study) can help you figure out if that's the right path for you.

### The Real Access Gap: Rural and Low-Population States

The states clustered in the middle and lower-middle of the table tell a more specific story: Vermont, South Dakota, Wyoming, Hawaii, New Mexico, and Maine combine genuinely low clinic counts (single or low double digits) with geography that makes travel to the nearest center a real burden. Unlike Texas or Florida, where the issue is competition for appointments, these states often have entire regions with no nearby sleep center at all.

This is where the gap is most consequential. A resident of a rural county in these states might face a multi-hour drive for an in-lab sleep study, an obstacle that leads many people to simply defer getting evaluated. For these residents, home sleep testing and telemedicine consultations aren't just a convenience, they're often the realistic path to getting diagnosed at all. Our guide on [telemedicine for sleep disorders](/blog/telemedicine-for-sleep-disorders) covers exactly which parts of the process can be handled remotely versus which still require a trip to a physical lab.

### Highest Apparent Density: Mississippi, Alaska, Alabama, Montana

At the top of the per-capita list, Mississippi, Alaska, Alabama, and Montana all show relatively high clinics-per-100k figures. For Mississippi and Alabama, this partly reflects the significant regional burden of obesity-related obstructive sleep apnea in the Southeast, which has driven meaningful investment in sleep medicine capacity even in moderately sized metro areas. For Alaska and Montana, low population combined with even modest clinic counts produces a high per-capita number, though this can still mask real in-state travel distances given the sheer geographic size of both states, a high per-100k figure doesn't fully capture how far apart those clinics might actually be.

### The AASM-Accreditation Gap Within States

Look closely at the "AASM-Accredited" column relative to total listings, and another pattern emerges: some states have a large gap between total clinics and accredited ones. Connecticut, for example, lists 104 clinics but only 51 are AASM-accredited, roughly half. Maine shows a similar pattern (24 listed, 11 accredited). Compare that to states like Idaho, Montana, and North Dakota, where every listed clinic in our directory carries AASM accreditation.

This matters beyond raw counts: a state can look well-served on paper while a meaningful share of its "sleep centers" haven't gone through independent quality verification. If you're evaluating options in a state with a wide accreditation gap, it's worth being more deliberate about checking credentials directly. Our guide on [why AASM accreditation matters](/blog/why-aasm-accreditation-matters) explains exactly what that credential verifies and why it's worth confirming before you book.

## Why Access Varies So Much, Beyond the Numbers

A few structural factors explain most of the state-to-state variation:

**Rural geography.** Sleep labs require dedicated overnight facilities, specialized equipment, and trained technologists on-site. That's a viable business and staffing model in a metro area; it's much harder to sustain in a county with a small, dispersed population. This is the single biggest driver of the gaps in states like Wyoming, Vermont, and South Dakota.

**Regional health burden.** States with higher rates of obesity, a major risk factor for obstructive sleep apnea, tend to see more clinical demand, which in turn supports more specialist capacity over time. This partly explains the Southeast's relatively strong per-capita numbers.

**Growth outpacing infrastructure.** Fast-growing Sun Belt states (Texas, Florida, Arizona, Nevada, Colorado) have added population faster than specialist training pipelines can keep up, producing longer waits even in states with substantial total clinic counts.

**Healthcare system density.** States with more academic medical centers and larger hospital networks tend to have more subspecialty sleep medicine physicians, since fellowship training programs are concentrated in these systems. Our guide on [what board certification in sleep medicine requires](/blog/board-certified-sleep-medicine-physician) explains why that specific training pathway matters, and why it's not evenly distributed geographically.

## What This Means If You're in a Gap State

If your state or region shows thin coverage, a few practical steps can help:

1. **Ask your doctor whether a home sleep test is appropriate for you.** For straightforward suspected obstructive sleep apnea, this avoids the need to travel for an in-lab study entirely. See our guide on [home sleep tests vs. in-lab studies](/blog/home-sleep-test-vs-in-lab-sleep-study) to understand which category you likely fall into.
2. **Look into telemedicine sleep programs**, which can handle the consultation, testing coordination, and ongoing CPAP management remotely, reserving an in-person trip only for the cases that genuinely require it.
3. **Check neighboring states or nearby metro areas.** If you're near a state line, the nearest accredited center may be a shorter drive across the border than deeper into your own state.
4. **Confirm accreditation and board certification specifically**, since, as shown above, not every listed clinic has the same level of independent verification. Our guides on [AASM accreditation](/blog/why-aasm-accreditation-matters) and [board-certified sleep physicians](/blog/board-certified-sleep-medicine-physician) explain exactly what to check.
5. **Don't let distance be the reason you skip evaluation.** Untreated sleep apnea and other sleep disorders carry real, cumulative health risks. A longer trip for an accurate diagnosis is worth it compared to years of unmanaged symptoms.

## The Bottom Line

Sleep health access in America isn't evenly distributed. Large, fast-growing states often have plenty of total clinics but face capacity strain from population growth. Rural, low-population states frequently have real geographic gaps where the nearest sleep center is hours away. And within any given state, the share of clinics carrying meaningful accreditation varies more than most patients realize.

Wherever you live, the data above is a starting point, not the final word: use it to understand what you're working with, then use tools like home sleep testing, telemedicine, and careful credential-checking to close the gap yourself.
