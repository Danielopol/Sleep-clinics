If you have sleep apnea and excess weight, you have probably seen tirzepatide advertised as a fix. Some of that is accurate and some of it is not, and the difference matters because the products sold under the same drug name are not the same thing.

Here is the accurate part. In December 2024 the FDA approved Zepbound, a brand of tirzepatide, to treat moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. It was the first drug approved for the condition. The approval rests on two 52 week trials in which people on the drug had large reductions in apnea events.

Here is what is easy to miss. That approval covers Zepbound, made by Eli Lilly. It does not cover compounded tirzepatide, which some telehealth services sell, and it does not mean tirzepatide replaces CPAP. This guide covers who may qualify, what the evidence shows, what it costs, how treatment works, and where the telehealth route differs from the approved one.

Prices and policies were checked on October 21, 2026. This is general information, not medical advice, and a clinician has to decide whether any of this applies to you.

## The short answer

- **Approved use.** Zepbound is approved for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity, used with a reduced-calorie diet and more physical activity.
- **Who qualifies.** The label's trial population had an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) of 15 or higher and a body mass index (BMI) of 30 or higher. A clinician confirms both.
- **What it did in trials.** After a year, average AHI fell by about 25 to 29 events an hour, compared with about 5 on placebo, and average body weight fell about 18 to 20 percent.
- **What it costs.** Lilly's self-pay prices for the OSA maintenance doses are as low as $449 a month with on-time refills, and $699 otherwise. Medicare Part D plans may cover it for sleep apnea. Insurance varies.
- **It does not replace CPAP.** The AASM says Zepbound reduces OSA severity but does not replace CPAP or other OSA treatments, and the trials did not test stopping CPAP.
- **Compounded tirzepatide is a different product.** It is not FDA-approved and was not what the trials tested.

## What the FDA approved

On December 20, 2024, the FDA approved Zepbound (tirzepatide) for the treatment of moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity, to be used with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity. The FDA called it the first drug treatment option for certain patients with obstructive sleep apnea.

Tirzepatide works by activating two gut hormone receptors, GLP-1 and GIP, which reduces appetite and food intake. The FDA states that by reducing body weight, studies show Zepbound also improves OSA, and that the improvement is likely related to the weight reduction. In other words, it is a weight-loss medication that helps sleep apnea because excess weight narrows the airway, not a drug that acts on the airway directly.

The same drug is sold as Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes. Zepbound is also approved for weight management, which is a separate indication.

It is for obstructive sleep apnea only. The AASM's patient guidance says it is not used for central sleep apnea. If you are not sure which type you have, our guide to [central versus obstructive sleep apnea](/blog/central-vs-obstructive-sleep-apnea) explains the difference.

## Who may qualify

The label's trials and the AASM's guidance describe the same criteria.

**For the sleep apnea indication:**

- You are an adult.
- You have moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea, meaning an AHI of 15 or higher events an hour, confirmed by a sleep test.
- You have obesity, meaning a BMI of 30 or higher.

Both numbers matter, and both come from clinical measurement. A [home sleep test or an in-lab study](/blog/home-sleep-test-vs-in-lab-sleep-study) gives the AHI, and your clinician measures your BMI.

**Who should not take it.** The label contraindicates Zepbound for anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or with Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2, and for anyone with a serious allergy to tirzepatide. It carries a boxed warning about thyroid C-cell tumors seen in rats. It should not be combined with other tirzepatide products or other GLP-1 medicines, and it is not recommended in people with severe gastroparesis. The AASM adds that birth control pills may work less well and should be discussed with a doctor.

If you have other conditions, take other medicines, are pregnant or planning to be, or have a history of pancreatitis or gallbladder problems, those are conversations for your prescriber. This list is not a screening tool.

## What the trials showed

The approval is based on two randomized, placebo-controlled trials of 52 weeks, run as one program called SURMOUNT-OSA. Together they enrolled 469 adults with moderate to severe OSA and obesity, and excluded people with type 2 diabetes. Participants were on average about 50 years old with a mean BMI near 39 and a baseline AHI near 50 events an hour, which is severe.

One trial enrolled people who were unable or unwilling to use PAP. The other enrolled people who were already on PAP. Everyone received diet and activity counseling, and tirzepatide doses were escalated to the highest tolerated of 10 mg or 15 mg weekly.

| At 52 weeks, tirzepatide vs placebo | Trial without PAP | Trial with PAP |
|---|---|---|
| Change in AHI (events per hour) | -25.3 vs -5.3 | -29.3 vs -5.5 |
| Reached remission or mild, non-symptomatic OSA | 42.2% vs 15.9% | 50.2% vs 14.3% |
| Change in body weight | -17.7% vs -1.6% | -19.6% vs -2.3% |

Numbers from the Zepbound prescribing information.

Some things the table does not say, and you should know:

- **Many people still had sleep apnea.** In the trial without PAP, about 42 percent reached remission or mild non-symptomatic OSA, which means about 58 percent did not. Results are averages, and individual responses vary widely.
- **It was a year of treatment with weight loss of nearly a fifth of body weight.** The AHI improvement tracks that weight loss, which is the FDA's own interpretation.
- **The trial population is specific.** Adults with obesity and moderate to severe OSA, without type 2 diabetes. The results do not automatically apply to people outside that group.
- **In the trial with PAP, participants paused it for a week before the final measurement.** The prescribing information states that the OSA studies did not evaluate the timing or appropriateness of stopping PAP in people who were previously using it successfully.

## How treatment works

Zepbound is a once-weekly injection under the skin of the abdomen, thigh or upper arm. The prescribing information describes the schedule for all uses:

- **Start at 2.5 mg once a week for 4 weeks.** This is a starting dose, not a maintenance dose.
- **Increase in 2.5 mg steps,** after at least 4 weeks on each dose, as tolerated, to reduce the risk of stomach side effects.
- **For sleep apnea, the maintenance dose is 10 mg or 15 mg once a week.** The maximum for any use is 15 mg.

Reaching a maintenance dose therefore takes at least several months. A person who needs 10 mg moves through 2.5, 5 and 7.5 mg first.

**Side effects.** The FDA lists nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal discomfort and pain, injection site reactions, fatigue, hypersensitivity reactions, burping, hair loss and gastroesophageal reflux disease. The label also warns of severe gastrointestinal reactions, so tell your prescriber about persistent or severe symptoms.

**Follow-up.** The AASM notes that regular follow-up visits and repeat sleep studies may be needed. A repeat test is how you find out whether your AHI has actually improved, which feeling better does not show. ## Tirzepatide and CPAP

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's patient guidance is direct: Zepbound helps reduce OSA severity but does not replace CPAP or other OSA treatments, and you should not stop CPAP or other therapy without medical advice. Its clinician guidance says the drug should be considered part of comprehensive care, alongside PAP, an oral appliance or surgery as appropriate, and that there is no head-to-head evidence comparing it with other OSA treatments.

That matches what the prescribing information says about the trials, which did not test stopping CPAP. Whether and when to reduce CPAP after weight loss is a decision for your sleep physician, based on repeat testing. Zepbound is approved to treat moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity, and in two one-year trials it cut apnea events substantially on average, alongside weight loss of nearly a fifth of body weight. It is a prescription medicine with real side effects and a boxed warning, it does not replace CPAP, and many people still had apnea after a year.
